Unlike humans, generative AI has no hands to feel water.

Yet in September 2026, OpenAI announced a claimed solution to the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize Problem, about the behaviour of fluids. The claim is extraordinary. But producing a proof and giving people an understanding of it are different achievements. [1]

For centuries, science has been a human conversation: ask, test, explain, challenge. As AI takes on more of that work, I keep coming back to a question: what happens when the answers get ahead of our understanding?

Open the machine and see why

n × (n + 1)

Multiply a number by the next whole number. For 3, that is 3 × 4 = 12. For 11, it is 11 × 12 = 132. You now have a method you can inspect, challenge and reuse.

This is a deliberately simple analogy. A few examples alone do not uniquely determine a rule, and a mathematical proof is much more than pattern matching.

A correct answer is valuable. An explanation lets someone else build on it. Researchers Lisa Messeri and Molly Crockett warn that AI tools can foster an illusion of understanding: we may feel we understand more than we actually do. [2]

A question still open.

AlphaFold predicts protein structures from amino-acid sequences. That gives researchers a powerful starting point. It does not, by itself, tell us everything about how a protein behaves in a living system.

Here is the essay's image of human p53, a protein involved in preventing tumour growth. Its colours carry information that a beautiful shape can hide.

Read between the colours.

Explore a region, then ask what a lab still needs to find out.

More confidence in the local structure.

Blue indicates a more confident prediction of this region's structure. Confidence is useful evidence, but it is not experimental confirmation or a guarantee of biological function.

Orange indicates lower confidence. A flexible or disordered region may not have one fixed shape. Colour is about confidence, not whether a region is important.

Terwilliger and colleagues describe AlphaFold predictions as valuable hypotheses that accelerate, but do not replace, experimental structure determination. [3] That distinction matters: a prediction can guide an experiment without settling the science.

My concern is what we give up when we start treating predictive power as a substitute for understanding.

Whose idea was it?

Science depends on people sharing unfinished ideas. If a researcher cannot see where an AI-generated result came from, questions about credit and trust become harder to resolve.

After OpenAI announced its fluid-dynamics result, mathematician Tristan Buckmaster questioned whether his private prompts had influenced the work. OpenAI says its investigation found they could not have influenced the system, including through training. [1]

A similar approach, a question of provenance.

Buckmaster raised concerns about the relationship between his private work and OpenAI's result. His account is one side of a disputed attribution story, not proof that ideas were taken.

We do not need to settle that dispute to ask for better records. Keeping an auditable account of contributions helps researchers explain where an idea came from, while protecting confidential work.

Give AI a job.
Keep someone accountable.

I do not think science should abandon AI. Data analysis, code generation and repetitive tasks are exactly where assistance can buy researchers time to think.

But where would you draw the line? Set up your own research team below. Each choice is about who leads the task; a human-led task can still use AI tools.

My line is this: a named human should be able to explain a paper's central claim, accept responsibility, and disclose how AI was used. ICMJE guidance already places authorship and accountability with people. [4] Auditable records can help without making every confidential prompt public.

Researching this essay changed my view. I began with an instinct that using AI in science was like cheating on an exam. I came to see how much time useful tools can give back. What I still want to protect is our ability to understand what we are doing.

Unlike humans, AI has no hands to feel water.

Let's keep our
feel for science.
