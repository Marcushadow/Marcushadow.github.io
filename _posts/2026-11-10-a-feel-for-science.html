Who understands it, and who takes responsibility?

Unlike humans, generative AI has no hands to feel water.

Yet in September 2026, OpenAI announced that its AI had discovered something about fluid flow that the greatest human minds could not. The Navier–Stokes equations describe fluid behaviour; whether their solutions can “blow up” is one of mathematics' seven million-dollar Millennium Prize Problems. OpenAI claims its model has proven one such case, while mathematicians are still verifying it. [1] [2]

For centuries, science has been a human conversation: ask, test, explain, challenge. As AI takes on more of that work, I keep coming back to a question: what happens when the answers get ahead of our understanding? My argument is that AI should remain an assistant to human judgement, rather than spearhead autonomous research.

From human research
to AI monologue.

OpenAI's release of 722 AI-authored mathematics papers brings that question into focus. Oxford mathematician James Maynard said it remained “very difficult to really extract any human understanding” from its Navier–Stokes proof. Producing more research does not necessarily mean people understand more. [3]

The shift goes beyond mathematics. A study of biomedical abstracts estimated that at least 13.5% of the 2024 abstracts showed signs of LLM-assisted writing. Sakana AI's “AI Scientist” goes further: it generates ideas, runs experiments and produced a paper that passed a workshop's first round of peer review. Writing assistance and autonomous research place very different decisions in a machine's hands. [5] [6]

The medicines we take and the buildings we live in depend on science that someone can explain and check. Retraction Watch reported that OpenAI withdrew three of those mathematics preprints after flaws were found. That does not establish that opacity caused the errors. It does show how much human checking is still required. [9]

This is a deliberately simple analogy. A few examples alone do not uniquely determine a rule, and a mathematical proof is much more than pattern matching.

A correct answer is valuable, but someone must still understand what makes it correct. An explanation lets other researchers learn from it, challenge it and extend it. Lisa Messeri and Molly Crockett warn that AI tools can foster an illusion of understanding: we may feel we understand more than we actually do. [4]

A convincing shape.
A question still open.

AlphaFold predicts protein structures from amino-acid sequences, giving researchers a powerful starting point for work including drug discovery. I agree that this is an important advance. But predictions can disagree with experimental results, and a predicted shape does not fully explain how a protein behaves in a living system.

Here is the essay's image of human p53, a protein involved in preventing tumour growth. Its colours carry information that a beautiful shape can hide.

Blue indicates a more confident prediction of this region's structure. Confidence is useful evidence, but it is not experimental confirmation or a guarantee of biological function.

Orange indicates lower confidence. A flexible or disordered region may not have one fixed shape. Colour is about confidence, not whether a region is important.

Terwilliger and colleagues describe AlphaFold predictions as valuable hypotheses that accelerate, but do not replace, experimental structure determination. [11] That distinction matters: a prediction can guide an experiment without settling the science.

Scientists still need to understand the underlying mechanisms before applying results, especially when human lives are at stake. To me, trading understanding for raw predictive power means answering questions we no longer understand.

Whose idea was it?

Understanding is at risk, but so is trust. Publishing a data-use policy does not mean people understand it: Pew Research found that 67% of U.S. adults understood little or nothing about what companies do with their personal data. Cisco's 2024 survey found that 84% of generative-AI users worried that data they entered would become public. These surveys describe public concerns, rather than establishing what happened in any particular research dispute. [12] [13]

Mathematicians Tristan Buckmaster and Levent Alpöge had used AI tools while working on a related fluid-dynamics problem involving the Euler equations. After OpenAI announced its Navier–Stokes result, Buckmaster saw similarities to their approach and questioned whether his private prompts had influenced it. OpenAI says its investigation found no such influence. [1] [7]

A similar approach, a question of provenance.

Buckmaster questioned whether private prompts from his work with Alpöge influenced OpenAI's result. Similarities prompted a concern about credit; they do not establish that ideas were taken.

Even if OpenAI is cleared, the damage to trust may outlast the dispute. Science advances because people share unfinished ideas. If researchers fear those ideas could resurface under someone else's name, they may become less willing to contribute. The open collaboration science depends on could quietly wither. [14]

The line between
tools and authors.

I do not believe humanity is ready for AI to lead and conduct its own research. That does not mean science should abandon AI. Data analysis, code generation, literature searches and drafting can give scientists more time for scientific judgement. [10]

Academic journals should draw a clear line between computational assistance and autonomous research. My proposal is to require a named human who can explain each paper's central claim and accept responsibility for its impacts. As readers, we should also call out papers that slip through without proper human authorship. Making that expectation known helps protect science's explainability.

Researching this commentary changed my view. I began with an instinct that using AI in science was like cheating on an important exam. Learning how computational tools accelerate discovery made me more open to assistance, provided human understanding is not sacrificed.

As a student working towards becoming a computer scientist, and perhaps a researcher, I want to think beyond raw results. Technologies affect human lives. Making them inspectable and explainable is part of our responsibility to make them safe, reliable and useful to people.

Unlike humans, AI has no hands to feel water.
If we let it do our thinking for us, we may lose our feel for science too.

Let's keep our
feel for science.
